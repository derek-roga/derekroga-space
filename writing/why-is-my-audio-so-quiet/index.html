Why Is My Audio So Quiet?

Quiet audio has a cause, and the cause is always in one link of a short chain. Check each link in order, and you will find it in minutes.

The volume chain

Volume is not one knob. It is a chain of volume controls, each with its own level, and the sound you hear is limited by the quietest link in that chain. Turn any one link up to maximum and the audio is still quiet if another link is sitting at twenty percent. That is why "I turned the volume all the way up and it is still quiet" is the commonest complaint about quiet audio, and why it is almost never a broken device.

The chain, from the sound to your ears, is this: the source file or stream, the app playing it, the browser tab if it is playing in a browser, the system volume, the output device, and finally the speakers or headphones themselves. Each link can be quiet independently, and each has its own check. Work through them in order, and stop when the sound comes back.

  • Source. The recording, the stream, or the video file. Some recordings are simply made quiet, and no setting downstream can fix a source that was recorded at a whisper.
  • App. The player, the call software, the game, the editor. Most apps have their own volume slider, independent of the system volume.
  • Browser tab. A browser plays audio per tab, and a tab can be muted while the browser itself is not. A muted tab shows a small speaker icon, easy to miss.
  • System. The operating system's master volume, plus the per-app volume mixer that most systems keep separately. An app can be turned down in the mixer while the master volume is at full.
  • Output device. The speakers, headphones or earbuds you are listening on, and the output the system is actually using. A system can be sending audio to the wrong device entirely.

The order matters. Start at the end of the chain, the part closest to your ears, and work backwards toward the source. The checks near the end are the quickest, and they are the ones people miss most often.

The cause ladder

The output device is wrong, or quiet

Check which output the system is using. A laptop can be sending audio to a monitor's built-in speakers, to a Bluetooth speaker in another room, or to a headset that is not on your head, while the volume on the device you are actually listening to is fine. The fix: open the sound settings, look at the output device, and select the one you are listening on. Then check the volume on the device itself. Headphones and speakers with their own volume controls can be turned down independently of the system, and a speaker at ten percent will sound broken even when everything else is at full.

The system volume is low, or one app is turned down

Check the master volume first, then the per-app mixer. On Windows, the volume mixer shows a separate slider for every app that is currently playing audio. On macOS, the system volume is the main control, but individual apps still keep their own levels. The fix: raise the master volume, then open the mixer and check that the app you are listening to is not turned down there. This is the single most common cause of "everything is quiet except one app" and "one app is quiet and everything else is fine".

The browser tab is muted

If the audio is playing in a browser, check the tab. A muted tab shows a crossed-out speaker on the tab itself, and right-clicking the tab offers an unmute option. Browsers also remember muted sites, so a site muted months ago stays muted until you unmute it. The fix: unmute the tab, and check the browser's site settings if the same site is always quiet. This one catches people because the tab looks normal at a glance, and the mute icon is small.

The app volume is low

Most players, call software and games have their own volume slider, and many remember it between sessions. A slider left low in one session stays low in the next. The fix: open the app's own settings or player controls and check its volume. In call software, check both the playback volume and the input level of the other side, because a quiet caller is not a fault on your end. In games, check the master volume and the individual sliders for music, effects and dialogue, which are often set separately.

The source is quiet

If every link in the chain is at full and the audio is still quiet, the source itself is quiet. Some recordings are made at a low level. Some video files have dialogue mixed far below the music. Some streams are quieter than others. The fix is different from the checks above, because there is no setting to turn up: the audio needs to be raised, which is the subject of the companion post, Boost Volume Without Distortion. Before going there, test with a different source. If a different recording plays at normal volume, the first recording is the problem, not your setup.

An enhancement is making it quieter

Some systems and apps apply loudness normalisation, night mode, or dialogue enhancement, and some of these make audio quieter rather than louder. Loudness normalisation turns loud recordings down to match a target level, which is fine until it turns an already-quiet recording down further. Night mode compresses the dynamic range and can make quiet passages harder to hear. The fix: check the sound settings for enhancements, normalisation, or spatial audio, and turn them off one at a time to see if the volume comes back. If it does, leave the culprit off.

When the recording itself is quiet

A quiet recording is a different problem from a quiet chain, and it needs a different fix. Turning up the volume on a quiet recording raises the noise floor along with the signal, so the result is louder and hissier, not cleaner. The proper fix is to raise the level of the recording itself, carefully, without clipping: normalise it, or raise the gain in stages and check for distortion at each step. That is a how-to in its own right, and it is covered in the distortion-free volume guide, which explains gain staging, normalisation and compression in plain language, and when boosting cannot help at all.

One quick test before you go there: play the same recording on a different device, a phone or another computer. If it is quiet there too, the recording is the problem. If it plays fine on the other device, something in the first device's chain is still turned down, and the ladder above will find it.

When it is a hardware fault

Sometimes the cause is physical, and no setting will fix it. The signs are consistent and easy to test. Audio that is quiet in one ear only, or that cuts in and out when the cable moves, points at a damaged cable or a failing jack. Audio that is quiet on every source, on every app, with every volume at full, and that stays quiet on a different pair of headphones, points at the output itself. Distortion that appears at low volume, not just at high volume, points at a damaged speaker or driver.

Test in this order: a different pair of headphones or speakers on the same device, then the same headphones on a different device. If the fault follows the headphones, the headphones are the problem. If it follows the device, the device's output is the problem. If it follows neither, and the same recording is quiet everywhere, the recording is the problem, and the boosting guide is the next step.

One last check, because it catches more people than any hardware fault: make sure nothing is covering the speaker. A laptop on a soft surface, a phone in a case with a blocked grille, or a speaker pushed against a wall can all sound broken when they are simply muffled. Move the device, clear the grille, and test again before concluding anything is faulty.
